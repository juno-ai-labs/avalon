There is a moment with every tool you use for long enough. The hammer stops
being a hammer and becomes your hammer. You know where it balances. You stop
looking at it. The old computer manuals understood this, which is why so many of
them ended with an appendix of key commands printed on a card you could tear out
and prop against the monitor. By that point in the book you had stopped learning
what the program did and started wanting it to do it faster.

You have reached that point with Rally. You can start a Canvas, ask Rally for
the next version, connect your tools and leave a Sidekick
running overnight. This chapter is about the last stretch: making Rally look and
behave the way you like, and getting it to answer to your fingers instead of
your mouse.

## Where settings live, and how to find them

**Some settings are about you, and some are about the device in front of
you.** Your name, your avatar colour and your time zone belong to your account.
Set them once and they follow you to every browser and computer you sign in on.
Light or dark, and whether this browser shows notifications, belong to the
browser you set them in. When a change does not seem to have reached your other
computer, this is usually why.

If you also use the [desktop app](../08-rally-everywhere/04-the-desktop-app.md),
it has a few more settings of its own (the menu bar icon, starting at login,
the app icon and how fast updates arrive). They sit in Settings under a
separate heading, **Desktop app → Application**, which the web app does not
show.

**You do not need to know where a setting lives.** Settings has its own search
box, and it knows the everyday words people type. Type "theme", "colour" or
"startup" and it finds the right switch, even when the switch is labelled
something else. If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember to
open Settings and type what you are looking for.

## What is ahead

The chapter starts with a tour of Settings and your profile, then covers the
look of the app, from light and dark to, in the desktop app, a Dock icon
called Ludicrous speed. Two
short pages are for the desktop app only: its habits as a Mac app (the menu
bar, starting at login, why closing the window does not quit it) and how it
keeps itself up to date. The web app needs neither. It is always the newest
version, every time you load it.

Then comes the card you could tear out: every keyboard shortcut Rally knows, in
tables, grouped by where your cursor is when you press them.

The last page is for people who read the appendix first. It is a roundup of
hidden corners: small tricks spread across the whole app, each with a pointer
to the page that explains it properly. Nothing on it is essential. All of it
saves a few seconds, and a few seconds a few hundred times a week is how a tool
becomes yours.
